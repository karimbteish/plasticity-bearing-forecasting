\section{Problem and motivation}
A model that monitors a machine has to keep learning, because the machine changes as it wears. But a neural network that is trained for a long time can slowly lose its ability to learn new patterns. This is called \emph{loss of plasticity}~\citep{dohare2024}.

\textbf{Question.} When a bearing degrades, should a vibration forecaster keep updating its weights, refresh part of them, or start over? We compare simple ``plasticity-preserving'' updates with ordinary continual training and with periodic retraining from scratch, at a similar computing cost. A secondary question is whether signals inside the network can tell us \emph{in advance} when a refresh will help.

\textbf{Task.} We predict the vibration level one hour ahead: the logarithm of the RMS vibration amplitude, $y_t=\log_{10}\rms(t+1\,\mathrm{h})$, from current and past vibration features. This is a regression task. It is not a failure alarm or a remaining-life estimate.

\textbf{Measurable goal.} A plasticity-preserving update succeeds if it has a lower mean absolute error (MAE) than both ordinary updating and periodic retraining on the held-out run. We also compare against simple ``persistence'' forecasts and check errors near the end of each bearing's life. We do not assume that any method will win.

\section{Impact}
The practical decision is whether a monitoring model should keep its weights, refresh some of them, or restart. A positive result would show maintenance teams which option gives the best accuracy for the computation spent. A negative result is also useful: it would show that resets can destroy useful knowledge, or that easier training does not always give better forecasts.

The most harmful error is \emph{underprediction}, which means forecasting less vibration than actually occurs while a bearing is failing. We therefore report it separately. Our data come from one laboratory rig with few failures, so the results cannot prove industrial safety. No maintenance decision will be automated.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Warm starts.} \citet{ash2020} showed that a network trained further from an old solution can generalise worse than one trained from scratch, even with the same training loss. They proposed \emph{shrink-and-perturb}: shrink the weights and add small random noise. We test this method on a forecasting task that arrives over time.

\textbf{Loss of plasticity.} \citet{dohare2024} showed plasticity loss in long image and reinforcement-learning experiments. Their fix, continual backpropagation, re-initialises rarely used neurons. Their work motivates our fair, equal-budget comparisons. We do not assume the effect will appear on bearing data.

\textbf{Inactive neurons.} \citet{sokar2023} linked a growing number of ``dormant'' (almost always inactive) neurons to lost capacity, and proposed ReDo, which resets them. We test a ReDo-style reset on regression.

\textbf{Interpreting internal signals.} \citet{lyle2023} found that plasticity loss can happen even without inactive neurons, so dormant neurons alone do not prove the mechanism. A recent preprint~\citep{wang2026} showed that some common internal measures fail to predict whether a network can still learn. We therefore test internal signals against a simple alternative: recent forecast error.

\textbf{Closest bearing work.} \citet{yang2026} add selective re-initialisation to a deep model for remaining-life prediction on other bearing datasets, and report lower errors. To our knowledge, they do not compare against periodic retraining at an equal budget, or test whether internal signals predict when a reset helps. Those two comparisons are our contribution. We do not claim a new reset method.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Source.} We use the IMS Bearing Data Set, collected by the University of Cincinnati's Center for Intelligent Maintenance Systems with Rexnord and distributed by NASA~\citep{ims,qiu2006} (\url{https://data.nasa.gov/dataset/ims-bearings}). NASA asks users to acknowledge the source; no other licence terms apply to our use.

Four bearings on one shaft were run until some of them failed, in three separate runs (Tests 1--3). Every 10 minutes, an accelerometer recorded a one-second vibration snapshot of each bearing. This gives 12 bearing histories, but only three runs and four failures. Bearings on the same shaft are not independent.

\textbf{Processed features.} We start from feature tables published by a public repository that processed the original recordings~\citep{features}. We confirmed that our files are identical to that repository's. In week 1 we will recompute the features ourselves from the original NASA recordings and compare them; our extraction script is ready.

\textbf{Inputs and preprocessing.} Each snapshot is summarised by 20 features: vibration amplitude, measures of signal shape, and the energy at the frequencies where specific bearing faults appear. We add a short moving average of each feature, giving 40 inputs. The first part of each bearing's history is used only to set the scaling of each feature, and is not used for training or scoring. We only form forecast pairs within continuous recording, so gaps are never filled in. We use one accelerometer per bearing.

\textbf{Limitations.} The data have recording gaps (Table~\ref{tab:data}), measurement noise, one operating condition and few failures. A bearing that did not fail is not a certified healthy control.

\section{Models and preliminary results}
\textbf{Simple baselines.} \emph{Persistence} forecasts that the vibration level in one hour equals the latest value, or the average of the last six. \emph{Ridge regression}~\citep{ridge} predicts the change from that average using the 40 features. It tests whether the features add any information. We train on Test 1 and select settings on Test 2.

\textbf{Neural network.} We implemented a small two-hidden-layer network from scratch in NumPy~\citep{numpy}, including backpropagation and the Adam optimiser~\citep{adam}, and checked the gradients numerically. It predicts the same change as ridge regression.

\textbf{What the baselines show.} Ridge and the network are only slightly better than persistence overall, and the simplest persistence forecast is best near failure (Table~\ref{tab:base}). The network's validation error was lowest after its first epoch: training longer only fitted Test 1 more closely. Persistence is therefore a strong baseline, and the network must be improved before the main comparison.

\textbf{Four update strategies.} As new data arrive, the network is updated in one of four ways:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Ordinary updating}: keep training the same weights.
\item \emph{Periodic retraining}: regularly reset all weights and retrain.
\item \emph{Shrink-and-perturb}~\citep{ash2020}: $\theta\leftarrow\lambda\theta+\sigma\varepsilon$, which shrinks the weights $\theta$ and adds fresh random noise $\varepsilon$.
\item \emph{ReDo-style reset}~\citep{sokar2023}: re-initialise only the inactive neurons.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Fair, causal updates.} Every few hours, each model trains on the most recent examples \emph{whose future values have already been observed}, so no method sees the future. All strategies get the same examples and the same number of training steps. We log the computation each one uses.

\section{Evaluation}
\textbf{Split.} Test 1 trains the starting model. Test 2 selects all settings. Test 3 is used only once, after every setting is fixed. We have looked at summary statistics of Test 3, but have not scored any model on it.

\textbf{Metrics.} We report MAE per bearing and overall, MAE near failure, average underprediction, and computing cost (run time, number of updates and resets). Each method is run with five random seeds, and we report the differences between methods with their spread. The comparison targets are the baselines in Table~\ref{tab:base} and the two reference strategies (ordinary updating and periodic retraining). Ablations change one design choice at a time, such as how often models update.

\textbf{Secondary study: can internal signals predict a helpful reset?} At regular checkpoints we copy the ordinarily updated model and continue it in two ways: as is, or after a reset. We then measure which copy forecasts the next day better. Before each copy, we record two internal signals: the fraction of inactive neurons, and how varied the neurons' outputs are (effective rank~\citep{roy2007}). We test whether these signals predict the benefit better than recent forecast error does.

\textbf{If plasticity loss does not appear.} We will first check on a small synthetic task that our code can detect plasticity loss at all. If the bearing data show little loss, we will report that as a result. The core deliverable is the forecasting comparison; a second bearing dataset is optional.
